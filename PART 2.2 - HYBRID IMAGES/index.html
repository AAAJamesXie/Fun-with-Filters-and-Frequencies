01 / Three hybrid images

View the larger versions up close, then step back or compare the smaller versions. The sources below are before alignment; the final images use the aligned common crop. Viewing distance, screen density and eyesight affect how clearly the interpretations separate. These are three constructed examples, not results from a perceptual user study.

Derek + Nutmeg

Nutmeg's whiskers and fur dominate the fine detail at larger display sizes, while reducing the image brings back Derek's broad face and skin-tone structure. The two eyes align, but the human mouth and cat muzzle have different geometry, so the large image still contains a blended facial structure rather than a perfectly natural cat portrait.

Aligned crop: 466 × 416 · σ low = 10 px · σ high = 4 px · high-band gain = 1.2. Before display clipping, 1.01% of RGB channel samples lie outside [0, 1].

Obama + Trump

The large view carries Trump's fine eye, forehead and mouth structure; smaller views emphasize Obama's broad face and smile. The change is less clean than the cat/owl example because mouth shape, hairline and clothing do not all coincide after aligning only the eyes. Both identities can remain partly visible at intermediate scales.

Aligned crop: 480 × 560 · σ low = 14 px · σ high = 2 px · high-band gain = 2.4. Before display clipping, 0.42% of RGB channel samples lie outside [0, 1].

Chelsea + Owl

This is my favorite example. At the larger size the owl's beak, circular eye rims and feather texture are prominent. Reducing the same image makes Chelsea's cat nose, warm face and broad eye structure more apparent. This identity contrast is easier to distinguish than the earlier cat/tiger pair. A faint cat-mouth ghost remains near the beak, so the large view is still a hybrid rather than a natural unaltered owl photograph.

Aligned crop: 477 × 288 · σ low = 10 px · σ high = 2 px · high-band gain = 1.5. Before display clipping, 0.16% of RGB channel samples lie outside [0, 1].

02 / Full process: Chelsea + Owl

I selected this self-chosen pair because the eyes provide corresponding landmarks while the owl's beak and feather structure differ clearly from the cat's nose and fur. The cat supplies the low band and the owl the high band. After comparing gains from 1 to 2, I increased high-band gain from the trial value of 1 to 1.5 for more visible owl detail, while keeping σ low = 10 and σ high = 2 fixed.

Alignment before filtering

Each source is EXIF-corrected and resized proportionally to width 512 with Lanczos resampling. I manually mark two eye centers in these prepared images and solve a two-point similarity transform for translation, rotation and uniform scale. Both sources are warped with bilinear interpolation to a shared 480 × 560 canvas. A separately warped support mask identifies valid source pixels; the largest rectangular region valid in both images is cropped before filtering.

For this pair, the common crop is 477 × 288 pixels. The transform maps the chosen eye coordinates to their common targets with residual below 5.68e-14 pixels. This verifies the coordinate calculation, not the anatomical accuracy of manually selected landmarks: the cat's mouth and the owl's beak do not fully coincide. No face recognition or nonrigid face morph is used. The method follows the two-point alignment principle in the supplied MATLAB starter, implemented in Python.

Low-pass, high-pass, combine

L = GσL * A   ·   H = B − GσH * B
Hybrid = L + gH

The normalized Gaussian is applied separably along x and y, independently for each RGB channel, using radius ceil(4σ) and symmetric boundaries. The high-pass filter is the impulse minus the Gaussian. The low band retains broad appearance, while the signed high band carries edges and texture. The gain g scales only the high band; the raw result is preserved without clipping. PNG output clips to [0, 1] and rounds to uint8, and this display conversion is identical for all color comparisons.

Hybrid implementation
def make_hybrid(low_image, high_image, sigma_low, sigma_high, gain=1., mode='gray'):
    low_image, high_image = np.asarray(low_image, dtype=float), np.asarray(high_image, dtype=float)
    if low_image.shape != high_image.shape or low_image.ndim != 3 or low_image.shape[2] != 3:
        raise ValueError('Aligned images must have equal RGB dimensions.')
    if mode not in MODES or not np.isfinite(gain) or gain < 0:
        raise ValueError('Invalid color mode or high-frequency gain.')
    low_input = low_image if mode in ('low_color', 'both_color') else np.repeat(luminance(low_image)[:, :, None], 3, axis=2)
    high_input = high_image if mode in ('high_color', 'both_color') else np.repeat(luminance(high_image)[:, :, None], 3, axis=2)
    low = gaussian_blur(low_input, sigma_low)
    high = high_input - gaussian_blur(high_input, sigma_high)
    return {'low_input': low_input, 'high_input': high_input, 'low': low,
            'high': high, 'hybrid': low + gain * high}

These are transforms of the two aligned inputs, the two filtered components and the raw hybrid. The inputs use the same grayscale convention as the hybrid calculation. Crucially, the high-pass FFT uses the signed high-frequency array, not its middle-gray visualization; the final FFT uses the unclipped hybrid. The numerical floor prevents log(0).

DC is centered. Frequency runs approximately from −0.5 to +0.5 cycles/pixel along each axis; moving away from the center increases spatial frequency. All five plots share a log display range of [-3.209, 5.614], selected from pooled 1st and 99.8th percentiles. Values outside that range are clipped only for visualization. The low-pass result concentrates energy near the center; the high-pass result suppresses the central low frequencies while retaining more outer detail. The hybrid carries both bands. Finite cropping, boundary extension and the logarithmic display leave leakage and residual structure; an ideal perfectly circular band separation is not expected.

Choosing the cutoffs

For a continuous Gaussian, G(f) = exp(−2π²σ²f²). Following the paper's half-amplitude convention, the approximate cutoff is f½ = √(2 ln 2)/(2πσ), in cycles/pixel; the complementary high-pass reaches half gain at the same frequency. Multiplying by the aligned crop width expresses it in cycles per image width. The finite sampled kernels approximate these cutoffs and do not have a hard frequency boundary. Increasing σ low lowers the low-pass cutoff; increasing σ high lowers the complementary high-pass cutoff and admits broader structures into the high band.

Derek + Nutmeg

Sigma low = 10 suppresses much of Derek's facial detail, and sigma high = 4 retains enough of Nutmeg's fur and muzzle structure to form a coherent near-view interpretation. The lower high-band sigma = 2 makes the cat finer and less dominant. Gain = 1.2 reinforces the cat detail without changing the low-band mean.

Selected approximate half-amplitude cutoffs: low = 8.73, high = 21.83 cycles per aligned image width.

Obama + Trump

Sigma low = 14 removes more of Obama's fine facial structure. Sigma high = 2 confines Trump's contribution to a narrower high-frequency band than sigma high = 3 or 4. A separate gain trial led to gain = 2.4 to restore sufficient high-frequency visibility; the narrower band improves the small-scale separation compared with simply adding broader Trump structure.

Selected approximate half-amplitude cutoffs: low = 6.42, high = 44.97 cycles per aligned image width.

Chelsea + Owl

I keep sigma low = 10 and sigma high = 2. The narrower high band retains the owl's fine beak and feather edges while allowing more of the cat to return at reduced scale. Comparing gains 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75 and 2, I select 1.5: owl detail is visibly stronger than in the gain-1 preview, while larger gains make the texture harsher. This boosts high-band amplitude by 50% without lowering its cutoff and admitting more owl structure. The cutoff contact sheet holds gain fixed at 1.5.

Selected approximate half-amplitude cutoffs: low = 8.94, high = 44.69 cycles per aligned image width.

Swapping the favorite pair

With the owl in the low band, the broad facial disc and beak remain prominent even at small sizes, and cat detail does not cleanly replace that owl interpretation. Keeping the cat in the low band and owl detail in the high band produces the clearer intended near-owl versus small-cat change.

04 / Bells & Whistles: where should color go?

Mandatory extension · Four controlled variants

I compare grayscale, low-frequency color only, high-frequency color only, and color in both. The transforms, crop, filter parameters and high-band gain are fixed within each pair; only the channels converted to grayscale change. Grayscale uses fixed RGB weights and is replicated into three channels before combining. Processing uses normalized encoded RGB, without gamma linearization.

Favorite pair, full-size variants

Derek + Nutmeg

I keep color in the low-frequency Derek component and use grayscale for the high-frequency cat. Skin color supports Derek at reduced scale, while the cat's gray fur and whisker edges remain strong up close. Both-color adds some chromatic fringes without resolving the human/cat shape mismatch; high-only color has relatively weak broad color because high-pass filtering removes slow chromatic variation.

Obama + Trump

I select grayscale. Keeping Obama's skin and curtain colors in the low band makes his identity too persistent in the large view. High-only color is subtle, while both-color retains much of the same broad color bias and introduces colored edges. Grayscale makes the experiment depend more on shape and spatial scale.

Chelsea + Owl

I select low-frequency cat color with grayscale owl detail. Warm cat color helps the reduced image retain the cat's appearance while the beak and feather detail remain visible up close. Both-color changes the eye and feather chroma and can create colored edge conflicts; high-only color removes the broad cat color cue. All four color versions use the same gain of 1.5, alignment and cutoff settings.

Color can strengthen broad grouping cues or add useful detail, but can also make one source persist at the wrong viewing scale. No single color assignment is best for every pair. The comparisons above support the choices rather than treating color as an automatic improvement.

What I learned

Adding a low band to a high band is easy; creating two coherent interpretations depends on matching the geometry and controlling how much structure survives in each band. A similarity transform aligns eyes but cannot make different mouths or muzzles identical. Color and gain are useful controls, and intermediate-size ambiguity remains a meaningful limitation rather than a numerical filtering failure.
