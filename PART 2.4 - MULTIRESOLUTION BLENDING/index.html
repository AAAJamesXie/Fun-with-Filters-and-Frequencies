PART 2.4:
MULTIRESOLUTION
BLENDING

Blend fine detail over a narrow boundary and broad color over a wider one. Three photographic experiments test a straight seam and two irregular regions.

One mask, many transition widths

I reuse the handwritten Gaussian/Laplacian stacks from Part 2.3. Every level has the full aligned image resolution. No built-in pyramid function, downsampling or upsampling is used within the stack.

G_A = Gaussian stack(A)       G_B = Gaussian stack(B)
L_A[l] = G_A[l] - G_A[l+1]   L_B[l] = G_B[l] - G_B[l+1]
L_A[5] = G_A[5]              L_B[5] = G_B[5]
M = Gaussian stack(binary region)
C[l] = M[l] * L_A[l] + (1 - M[l]) * L_B[l]
result = sum(C[l] for l = 0..5)

White mask pixels select A; black selects B. Image scales are σ = [0, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32] pixels; mask scales are σ = [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32]. The finest mask is already softened by σ = 1; the sharp binary mask is retained for the hard-cut baseline. Successive blur kernels use √(σ²next − σ²previous) with radius ceil(4σ) and reflected boundaries. Discrete, finite kernels approximate the nominal Gaussian scales.

The final coarse residual is essential. Summing all six weighted levels reconstructs the blend directly; stacks need no expansion, and the weights already sum to one, so there is no extra division by two. This adapts the region-weighted algorithm on printed page 230 of Burt & Adelson (1983).

Preparation is separate from the stack. Mars/cookie use a black backdrop; basketball/orange use white. Foreground silhouettes are extracted and their edges softened slightly, then uniformly scaled and centered on 512 × 512 canvases. Kiwi/lime instead use three-point affine maps of their cut faces; the neighboring whole lime is removed. All six supplied files remain unchanged. Preparation and the labeled basketball color adjustment affect appearance independently of stack blending.

Stack processing uses float64 encoded RGB; this experiment does not linearize gamma or claim radiometric accuracy. Signed bands remain unclipped. Only exported display images are clipped to [0,1] and rounded to 8-bit.

Three custom blends

These are custom composites of existing photographs, not generated scenes. The first uses a straight vertical seam; the next two use different irregular polygons. Each case includes the mandatory color study.

Experiment 01 · vertical mask

Mars Cookie: an edible planet

A vertical step mask keeps Mars on the left and a chocolate-chip cookie on the right: an edible planet. Similar round silhouettes and warm brown colors connect two deliberately unrelated subjects. Mars is a photographic Viking mosaic.

Original photographs and alignment evidence

Prepared canvas: 512 × 512 pixels. Source-coordinate points, foreground bounds, affine matrices and polygon vertices are saved in the experiment record. The overlay uses selected inputs. Visual agreement is distinct from numerical reconstruction precision.

The hard cut exposes the central boundary. Single-scale feathering mixes surface detail over a broad strip; the multiresolution version gives fine craters and cookie texture a narrower transition while sharing broad tones. The outer silhouettes still differ: blending cannot make a perfect sphere.

Mandatory Bells & Whistles: color

The mask, geometry and scale schedule stay fixed. Only input color changes. Gray uses the fixed encoded-RGB weights 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722, repeated into three channels.

Both-color is selected for the warm planetary and baked-cookie tones. Gray keeps texture but loses the food cue. Coloring only one source makes a less coherent, partly gray imaginary object.

Experiment 02 · irregular mask

Basketball texture in an orange

An eleven-vertex irregular polygon inserts basketball texture into an orange. Both objects have a round shape and pebbled surface. Basketball is A; orange is B. Separate Lab chroma matching brings the pinker ball closer to the orange before the selected comparisons.

Separate preprocessing: basketball color

Lab a/b offset: [8.8125, 40.78125]. It is the difference between median B and A chroma in a shared central radius-150 region, excluding dark A pixels (minimum RGB ≤ 0.12). The offset is applied to A before blending. Lab L is fixed during this operation; conversion to RGB can clip out-of-gamut colors. The unadjusted and selected blends use identical geometry, masks and scales.

All three methods below use the same chroma-adjusted A and unchanged B. The hard cut reveals the polygon; feathering softens the ball lines. Multiresolution blending retains lines and texture, but a texture boundary remains visible. Color matching helps broad agreement independently of the blending algorithm.

Mandatory Bells & Whistles: color

The mask, geometry and scale schedule stay fixed. Only input color changes. Gray uses the fixed encoded-RGB weights 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722, repeated into three channels.

Both-color keeps the orange recognizable as fruit. A-only and B-only color expose the insertion through desaturation. Gray reduces chromatic disagreement but weakens the fruit identity. All four modes start from the selected chroma-adjusted inputs.

Experiment 03 · irregular mask

Kiwi seeds in a lime

A different eleven-vertex irregular polygon places a kiwi seed ring inside a lime while preserving the lime rind. Three manually selected points align the cut faces; the neighboring whole lime is removed during preparation.

Original photographs and alignment evidence

Prepared canvas: 512 × 512 pixels. Source-coordinate points, foreground bounds, affine matrices and polygon vertices are saved in the experiment record. The overlay uses selected inputs. Visual agreement is distinct from numerical reconstruction precision.

Favorite result: green flesh, circular cut faces and the lime rind support a readable hybrid fruit. A hard cut exposes the polygon; broad feathering weakens the seed ring. Multiresolution blending preserves more distinct seeds with a gentler broad-tone transition. Local texture changes remain around the insertion.

Mandatory Bells & Whistles: color

The mask, geometry and scale schedule stay fixed. Only input color changes. Gray uses the fixed encoded-RGB weights 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722, repeated into three channels.

Both-color is selected because the green tones connect kiwi flesh to lime. Gray loses this cue; kiwi-only color emphasizes a patch, and lime-only color desaturates the insertion. No chroma matching is applied to this pair.

Inside the favorite: kiwi + lime

The kiwi is source A and the lime is source B. The same irregular region is blurred separately at each scale. Fine-scale weights remain localized; coarse-scale weights spread farther into the lime image.

Masked frequency contributions

Rows below show weighted bands at levels 0, 2 and 4; the last row sums all six levels, including the unshown coarse residual at level 5. Each signed row uses one shared max-absolute scale across its three panels: display = 0.5 + band / (2 × row maximum). Gray means zero, darker values are negative. The bottom row uses ordinary RGB display clipping.

This is a full-resolution stack adaptation of the contribution visualization in Figure 10 of Burt & Adelson and Figure 3.42 in Szeliski. These are the weighted construction bands M·LA and (1−M)·LB, not the Laplacian stack obtained by filtering the final image afterward; spatial masking and filtering do not commute.

Mask-scale exploration

The image stacks and polygon stay fixed; these trials vary the entire mask schedule. The wider trial changes several scales at once, so it is a comparison of schedules, not an isolated experiment on the finest sigma.

The sharp trial risks retaining a more abrupt fine-detail border. The wider schedule spreads kiwi texture and low-frequency color over more of the lime; it is not automatically better. I select the middle schedule to keep the seed ring localized and recognizable without using an entirely sharp fine mask. These are visual judgments, not an objective quality ranking.

What I learned

The most important lesson is that a blend needs different transition widths for different spatial frequencies. A broad feather can soften color while also mixing incompatible detail. A stack lets fine texture switch over a narrow region while broad tones transition farther. Keeping the final residual makes reconstruction exact before display clipping.

Good masks and compatible geometry matter as much as filtering. Round silhouettes and similar colors make these combinations coherent. The lime rind supports the kiwi insertion; the basketball still exposes a texture transition. Color preprocessing must be shown explicitly and held fixed across method comparisons. Tiny reconstruction errors confirm numerical consistency, not visual realism.
